The knackery truck was coming to pick up Smokey on Friday at 7 in the morning. I'd sold him myself, for 400 dollars. 21 days later, Smokey pulled the cart up the hill from the creek flat on his own, with half a load of firewood. And the gel that saved him got me off the waiting list for a new hip.

My name is Harold Vickers. I'm 69 and I live on a small dairy block, about 40 acres, 12 kilometres out of Warrnambool, in south-west Victoria.

I've worked the land my whole life. Milking at 5 in the morning in the rain, cutting firewood, bringing the cows in with mud up to my knees. The weather down here doesn't forgive you. And your joints don't forget either.

Margaret and I have been married 44 years. The kids moved away for work, to Melbourne and to Geelong. Out here it's just the two of us, 9 cows, the dogs and Smokey.

Smokey is a work horse, a stock-horse cross, light grey, 18 years old. I bought him as a foal at a horse sale in Camperdown, with the money from a good potato crop. For 15 years he pulled the cart, dragged logs up from the creek flat, took me into town on Sundays and brought me home again through thick fog.

Last winter he started to fail.

First his front knees swelled up. Then his hocks. He struggled to get up in the mornings, and I'd find him lying in the mud in the yard, shaking, with sad eyes. One day in July he went down with the cart fully loaded, right in the middle of the hill. It took 3 neighbours to unhitch him.

I called the local vet. He checked him over, flexed his legs and took X-rays with a portable machine.

"Harold, this horse has no cartilage left in his knees. It's bone on bone. He won't work again, and he's going to suffer this winter. Best thing is to have him put down, or sell him to the knackery while he can still walk."

I knew he was right. Feed's dear in winter, hay was $15 a bale, and on a farm a horse that doesn't work is a cost nobody can carry.

A horse buyer from Colac offered me 400 dollars. I signed the paper. He was coming on Friday at 7 in the morning with the truck, to take him straight to the knackery.

I didn't sleep that week. Neither did Margaret. On Thursday afternoon I went out to the yard with a bucket of oats and stood leaning on the gate, looking at Smokey. My hip hurt from standing. Everything hurt him.

That night our granddaughter Emily rang. She's studying vet science in Melbourne. Margaret had told her.

"Pop, don't let him go. I did my placement with a vet who brings back horses everyone else has given up on. Dr. Daniel Mitchell. Let me ring him. If Smokey doesn't improve in a week, I'll pay you back whatever you lose with the buyer myself."

I told her there was no point. The paper was already signed. She said: "Pop, what have you got to lose by letting him have a look?"

Dr. Mitchell turned up at half past 10 that night, with a head torch and gumboots up to his knees. He didn't make me a single promise. He climbed into the yard, talked quietly to Smokey and felt all 4 legs, one by one, for half an hour.

"The vet's right about what he's seeing," he told me. "The cartilage is worn and the joints are dry. But dry doesn't mean finished. If we feed the joint from the outside and give it its fluid back, this horse will work for a few more years yet."

He took a tube of gel out of the ute.

"I've been using this for 15 years on farm horses, stock horses, dogs and cattle. It goes through the skin and straight into the joint. It feeds the cartilage, brings back the joint fluid, and the joint stops grinding on itself."

He rubbed the gel into his knees and hocks for a good while, hard, and then showed me how to do it myself. Morning and night, every day, no skipping.

At 6 in the morning I rang the buyer. I told him the horse wasn't going anywhere. He gave me an earful, but he didn't come.

Here's what happened.

Day 3: Smokey got up on his own in the morning. When I came out, he was already standing there, waiting for me.

Day 7: He walked from the shed out to the paddock, 200 metres, without limping.

Day 12: He trotted. Margaret came out to look with her apron still on and stood crying at the door.

Day 21: I hitched him to the cart with half a load of firewood and he pulled it up the hill from the creek flat on his own. The same hill where he'd gone down in July. At the top he stopped, snorted and looked at me as if to say, "Well?"

A month later the local vet came out to vaccinate the cows. He saw Smokey trotting in the paddock and went quiet. "Harold, is that the same horse?" He asked me 3 times what I'd put on him.

But this story isn't only about Smokey.

All those mornings, while I was bending down in the mud to rub a horse's knees, my own hip wouldn't give me a moment's peace.

My right hip had been bad for 4 years. To get into the ute I had to lift my leg in with both hands. Margaret put my shoes on for me, because I couldn't reach my feet. Walking behind the cows was torture, and at night I couldn't find a position: on my side it hurt, on my back it hurt.

My hands weren't working either. On frosty mornings I couldn't close them to milk, and my lower back locked up every time I lifted a 25-kilo bag.

The GP gave me Panadol and Voltaren. Then tramadol, which left me in a fog with an upset stomach. Once they gave me a cortisone injection in the hip. It lasted 7 weeks. Then the pain came back, as bad as before or worse.

The orthopaedic surgeon at the public hospital told me I needed a hip replacement. It's covered on Medicare, but they told me the waiting list was at least eighteen months. A private hospital in Geelong wanted 30,000 dollars. A small dairy farmer can't scrape that together even if he sells every cow he's got.

One afternoon Dr. Mitchell dropped in to check on Smokey. He found him trotting in the paddock and me limping along behind him. That's when I asked him what had been going round in my head for weeks.

"Doctor, is there something like this for people? Because my hip's worse than this horse's knees."
