Special report on joint health & mobility

“When walking becomes difficult… even family can start to pull away” Dr. Wade Brennan, a young Australian physician, travels aged care homes across Australia to give older adults who were left behind the thing they want most — movement in their joints, and the dignity of living on their own. Dr. Wade Brennan, only 30, resigned from a well-known private hospital in Sydney to do what almost no physician chooses to do — travel to aged care homes across Australia and help older adults their families left behind because they could no longer walk, stand, or take care of themselves. Working with a research team, he developed a way to care for the joint that helped older adults move again — without surgery, and without depending on medication for life.

We arrived on a Tuesday morning at an aged care home outside Sydney. The road in runs between narrow streets, old fences and barking dogs. “Are you looking for the young doctor?” the woman at the corner store asked. “He comes every Thursday. The older residents wait for him at the door. Some of them cry when they see him.”

That was the first time we heard the name Dr. Wade Brennan — a 30-year-old physician who resigned from a private hospital in Sydney to devote himself to aged care homes across Australia, caring for older adults their families had left behind.

The reason so many older adults are left behind is almost always the same.

They can no longer walk.

Knees worn down until a step is impossible. A back bent from bone degeneration. Hands and fingers so shaky they cannot hold a spoon.

And at the moment they need help most, many families decide the care is a burden — too hard, too expensive — and take them to an aged care home.

“My son told me, ‘Mum… I can’t keep helping you out of the chair every day. I’m exhausted.’ The next day he brought me here. Three years have passed, and he has never come back to visit.”— Dorothy Hale, 79, an aged care home outside Brisbane, Queensland
“When my knees became so bad that I could barely walk, my wife said she could not keep living with someone who needed this much help. My children sided with her. Eventually they brought me here, and they never came back.”— Frank Miller, 73, an aged care home in Perth, Western Australia

Dr. Wade Brennan:

People between 55 and 80 take the same painkillers and anti-inflammatories for years. The pills ease the pain for a few hours. They do not stop the joint from wearing out.

Meanwhile the cartilage keeps thinning, the joint dries out, and every movement gets harder.

Taken for years, those same pills can damage the stomach, the liver and the kidneys.

It becomes a cycle.

Pain → a pill → the pain returns → another pill → the joint keeps dying.

And almost no one tells them there is a way to care for the joint itself — applied where it hurts — instead of swallowing another pill that only covers the pain.

Knee, back or hip pain after 45 is not “just aging.” It is a sign the joint is wearing out and needs care at the cause.

Painkillers and anti-inflammatories only quiet the symptom. They do not restore a joint that is already degenerating. Even people who take them every day can keep getting worse if the cause is never treated.

Long-term use of painkillers or anti-inflammatories can raise the risk of side effects, including stomach, liver and kidney problems. Care should follow a doctor’s guidance, and it should fit the person — not a pill taken forever.

Dr. Wade Brennan:

One important point: even people who regularly use pain medication may continue to have mobility problems. Pain relievers are generally intended to reduce symptoms; they are not designed to rebuild damaged joint structures.

Long-term use of pain relievers or anti-inflammatory medicines may increase the risk of side effects in some people, including stomach, liver or kidney problems. Treatment decisions should be made with a qualified healthcare professional.

These are the symptoms Dr. Wade Brennan sees most often in aged care homes, and the ones many Australians dismiss:

  • Severe knee pain walking up or down stairs, or even shifting in a chair.
  • Morning stiffness. The body will not move for 30–60 minutes after waking.
  • Chronic back pain. Bending, or getting out of bed, is difficult.
  • Swollen knees, ankles or fingers.
  • A click or a grind in the joint with every movement.
  • Hip pain radiating down the thigh. A short walk, then they have to stop.
  • Misshapen fingers, enlarged knuckles, trouble holding anything.
  • Numbness or weakness in the hands or legs, which can come from a disc or a pinched nerve.
  • A stab of pain in the lower back every time they stand up.
  • A stooped back. The posture folds from degeneration of the spine.
  • Night pain that wakes them again and again. They cannot sleep.

These symptoms should not be ignored. Especially after 45–50. They can be the sign of a joint that is still degenerating. Without care at the cause, movement gets harder, daily life shrinks, and some people end up unable to live without help.

The longer joint pain is left without real care, the higher the chance that movement keeps getting worse. A lot of older adults lose the ability to live an ordinary day because they ignored the warning, or because they only covered the pain and never had the joint assessed.

If you or someone you love has chronic joint pain, do not wait until it takes over the day. Care, and a doctor’s look, early on can still leave a real option — including one that does not start with surgery or a pill for life.
