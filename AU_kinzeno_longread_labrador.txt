They were going to put him down. Twelve years looking after my family, waiting for me in the driveway at eleven at night — and the vet told me the kindest thing would be to put him to sleep. A yellow Labrador who never once let my family be alone.

My name is Gary Thompson. Everyone calls me Gaz. I'm 54 and I live in Adelaide. I've been driving taxis since I was 32 — twenty-two years working the same rank outside the Central Market. I've got my wife, Sharon, and three kids: Chloe, who's 17, the middle one who's 12, and the boy, who's 9.

And I had — I say had, because they nearly took him from me — Buster.

Buster turned up when Chloe was five. A passenger left him in my cab in a cardboard box without so much as asking. Yellow Labrador, almost golden, with a little face that would melt you. Chloe let out that little-girl scream, and from that day on he was her brother.

Twelve years later, Chloe's 17. Buster grew up with her, waited at the door for her, sat through every teenage drama. For me, he's my night-time mate — when I get home late, he's waiting in the driveway, wags his tail, sniffs my shoes and lies back down.

Twelve years without missing a night. And then, all of a sudden, he couldn't even get up.

Labradors get elbow problems — dysplasia, they call it. In big breeds the cartilage wears down, and Buster's 35 kilos. It started small: he couldn't get up in one go, then he started limping on the right side, then he didn't want to jump in the cab anymore. One day at dinnertime he didn't even get up at the rattle of the kibble bag.

I took him to Dr. Fitzgerald. X-rays, the full check. He called me into his consulting room.

"Gary, Buster has elbow dysplasia in both elbows, with secondary arthritis. Both joints are affected — the cartilage is gone. That hurts with every single step. At his age... the kindest thing would be to consider euthanasia."

Twelve years. The same dog who watched my five-year-old turn into a young woman of seventeen.

I said no. I bought the tablets — $200 a month. Three weeks, four weeks. Buster didn't get any better. One afternoon Chloe said to me, crying: "Don't put him down, Dad. Please."

I stood in the hallway feeling useless.

One arvo a grazier hopped in the cab — Akubra hat, leather boots, heading out to the bus for Murray Bridge. We got talking. I mentioned the dog.

That half a wag. That's what made me call.

Day 3: Same as before. No visible change. I kept going. Morning and night, never missed one.

Day 7: It was twenty to six in the morning, still dark. I heard a noise in the lounge room. Buster was standing up — on his own, no help — in the middle of the room, with that Labrador tail wagging for real, strong, like it used to. I lost my breath for a second. I texted Chloe, who was still asleep: "Buster got up on his own this morning." She replied with nothing but crying emojis and hearts.

Day 14: Buster got up the step to the driveway. The step he hadn't been able to climb for months. I watched from the kitchen — he walked up, hesitated, put his paw up and got up it in one go. He went straight to his empty bowl and looked at me as if to say, "Someone ought to fill this." Sharon put her hand over her mouth. Then she gave him a bit of toast.

Day 21: Buster went out into the backyard, did the whole lap, sniffed along the fence, came back inside. Like he always used to. He didn't run — he's twelve. But he walked. Four steady legs, no calculating, no limping.

Chloe came home from school and Buster went to meet her at the door. She knelt down and hugged him. I had to turn around because I don't like people seeing me like that.

Two months later, Dr. Fitzgerald checked him over: "Gary, I don't know what you've been putting on him, but this dog's doing better than I'd ever have expected with that diagnosis. His mobility has improved a lot."

Twelve years of a yellow Labrador waiting at that door. And we came that close to taking it away from him.

"Gary, you've got advanced joint wear in both knees. The cartilage is practically gone. It's bone on bone. If you want to keep working, you need a double knee replacement — two operations, six months apart. All up, roughly $50,000 in a private hospital."

Fifty thousand dollars. On a good day I make $250 to $300, and out of that comes fuel and the payments on the cab. That's more than I clear in a whole year. I didn't have it. And without the operation: a walking stick in two years, a wheelchair after that.

A cabbie in a wheelchair isn't a cabbie.

I drove home with that in my head and buried it. Kept working, kept putting up with it, taking 400s of ibuprofen like they were lollies.
